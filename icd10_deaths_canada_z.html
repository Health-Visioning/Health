Methodology: empirical rolling-window z-score

Earlier versions compared the 2020–2024 average to a flat 2000–2019 mean, then to a linear-trend projection — but the trend version's standard error assumed pre-pandemic years were statistically independent, which annual death counts generally aren't. The current method avoids that assumption by measuring variability directly from history: for every row, we fit a straight-line trend through 2000–2019, then look at all 16 overlapping 5-year windows that fit inside that span (2000–04, 2001–05, … 2015–19) and calculate how far each window's average actually fell from the trend line. That spread is the benchmark — however much real 5-year averages naturally wandered before the pandemic, whatever the cause. The 2020–2024 average is then scored as a z-score against that empirical spread, rather than against a formula's assumption about it.

One safeguard was necessary: a handful of rows (125 of roughly 260) have unusually smooth historical trends with very little natural year-to-year noise, which makes the empirical spread artificially tiny and can inflate a modest deviation into a triple-digit z-score. For those rows, the z-score's denominator is floored at a standard regression-theory estimate (accounting for the correct degrees of freedom and the extra uncertainty of extrapolating the trend forward to 2022) whenever that estimate is larger than the empirical one — so no row's flag can rest on an unrealistically small denominator.

What this table is, and isn't: roughly 90 of ~260 rows flag yellow or red. With that many comparisons running at once, a handful of yellow flags would appear by chance alone even if nothing pandemic-related had happened anywhere — so treat every flag as "worth a second look," not as a proven anomaly, and treat the strength of a red flag as suggestive rather than confirmatory. This is a screening tool built from crude, non-age-standardized annual counts — it's well suited to surfacing which sub-groups deserve closer investigation, not to making a statistically airtight causal claim about any one of them.

About the pandemic-period marker

Columns for 2020–2022 carry a purple top-border — Canada's national COVID-19 emergency response ran through this window, and it's the period the excess-mortality literature generally treats as pandemic-affected. It's a visual reference band only, not a statistical adjustment.

This table still cannot tell you whether COVID-19 vaccination influenced any of these counts. These are aggregate counts by ICD-10 cause-of-death chapter — there is no vaccination-status field anywhere in the source data, and no code here specifically captures a vaccine adverse event. A flagged row is far more parsimoniously explained by COVID-19 infection itself, its downstream health effects, and pandemic-era disruption to care than by vaccination — Health Canada's CAEFISS surveillance is the appropriate source for vaccine-specific safety signals, not this dataset.
