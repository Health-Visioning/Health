How this works, and how far to trust it
  • Methods. Log-linear (a constant percentage change per year) competes with EMA, exponentially weighted regression and rolling regression; straight-line fits are not used anywhere. The default comes from the size of the numbers (order 1, rates mostly under 10: the best local technique; orders 2 and 3: log-linear), and another technique replaces it only when its backtest error is at least 10% lower. Rates ended up with log-linear for 5, EMA for 104, exponentially weighted regression for 41, rolling regression for 38 of 188 codes; the order rule was overridden on fit for 9. Counts ended up with log-linear for 68, EMA for 81, exponentially weighted regression for 12, rolling regression for 26 of 187 codes; the order rule was overridden on fit for 119.
  • Steps need five years. A major step, up or down, restarts the baseline at the step, and at least 5 years of data must follow it. If the most recent step leaves fewer, the code is labelled cannot model for that measure. Projections are frozen at 2019 and never see 2020 data.
  • Typical error and gap ÷ error. Typical error is the root-mean-square relative error of the method's 1–5-year-ahead forecasts in backtests on pre-2020 data. Gap is the 2020–24 average against the projection's average, and gap ÷ error counts the gap in typical-error units. A gap inside one band is an ordinary forecast miss.
  • Tiers, and what they mean in standard errors. Gap ÷ error is not a z-score: in quiet pre-pandemic years the median code already reaches about 1 error band, so the bands are too narrow. Matching how often quiet years (2014–18 and 2015–19) reach each threshold to the chance of a normal z-score gives rough equivalents: watch (1.5 to 2) ≈ 1.0 standard errors, weak (2 to 3) ≈ 1.3, moderate (3+) ≈ 1.7, and strong (4+ in both measures) ≈ 2.0 in each measure. Quiet years reached those levels about 3 in 10, about 1 in 5, about 1 in 10 and about 1 in 20 respectively, so the watch and weak tiers are barely distinguishable from chance. Outside 2 bands, 2020–24 had 15% of counts and 18% of rates, against 17–22% in quiet years. Only the strong and moderate tiers deserve attention.
  • Counts and rates. The two measures agreed on flag or no flag for 166 of 187 codes, and all 20 of the codes flagged in both pointed the same way. Many counts series are matched best by a flat EMA, which cannot absorb the pre-2020 ageing trend, so counts gaps can include population growth and ageing. Across codes the median gap is similar (+1.4% for counts, +1.0% for rates), but individual codes differ.
  • Cannot-model codes. 5 codes fail in both measures (A41, F05, I63, R09, X40-X49) and 1 fails in one (R64 in counts). They include X40-X49 (accidental poisoning, which holds the opioid overdose deaths): its 2017 step leaves only 3 years of data, so no projection is made, and the rule cannot tell a real change from a coding change.
  • Driver families are hypotheses. They come from each code's ICD-10 range and direction of change, not from a test of cause. Family 8, "Not independently classified," holds codes that no driver rule covers (the assault codes X95 and X99, and Down's syndrome Q90) and four codes (L08, L89, Y86, J45) whose family would otherwise rest on their ICD-10 letter alone. R99 is a certification catch-all, not a disease.
  • COVID-19 displacement flag. A code is flagged when it ran below its projection by 2+ error bands in either measure and at least half of its 2015–19 deaths were at age 80 or over. "Persists" means the 2023–24 deficit is at least four-fifths of the 2020–22 deficit. A deficit that persists as COVID-19 deaths fell fits earlier deaths depleting the at-risk group better than same-year substitution, but fewer diagnoses during the pandemic would look the same, and these tables cannot separate them.
