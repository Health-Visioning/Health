Comparing Newfoundland and Labrador and British Columbia

What influences health disparities

Newfoundland and Labrador has some of the worst health outcomes in Canada — shorter life expectancy, higher rates of heart disease, diabetes, and cancer. British Columbia has some of the best. Both provinces were compared on risk factors, outcomes, and social context, then checked against published research on what actually drives that kind of gap.

What explains the variation

Bauer et al. (2015), validating the County Health Rankings model (University of Wisconsin Population Health Institute / Robert Wood Johnson Foundation) against actual U.S. county-level outcomes. This is a share of statistical variance explained, not a share of deaths caused, and it’s U.S. research — no Canadian equivalent exists at this resolution.

Social/economic 25%, health behaviors 18%, clinical care 9%, physical environment 2%, other/unexplained 46%.

Social and economic factors

25%

Income, education, employment, and the strength of a community’s social fabric. This is the largest driver in the model, bigger than health behaviors, because money and education shape almost everything downstream, including which behaviors are even accessible.

Example: median after-tax income was $65.7k in NL versus $73.9k in BC in 2023, and median family net worth was $334k versus $774k.

Health behaviors

18%

Smoking, diet, alcohol use, and physical activity levels. These are what most people picture first when they think about health risk, but the model ranks them second, behind social and economic conditions.

Example: 2023 smoking rates were 15.0% in NL versus 9.5% in BC; heavy drinking was 26.4% versus 16.6%.

Clinical care

9%

Access to care and quality of care once you get it: coverage, wait times, screening rates, having a regular provider. It carries less weight than most people expect, and Canada’s universal coverage may compress its effect further versus the U.S. data this model is built on.

Example: family doctor access breaks the usual pattern. 69% of NL residents have a regular provider versus roughly 70% in BC. BC struggles with this almost as much as NL does.

Physical environment

2%

Air and water quality, housing conditions, and the built environment: walkability, access to healthy food outlets. It’s the smallest measured slice, though it still touches the risk factors above it, since rural food deserts affect diet quality.

Example: 47% of NL’s population is rural versus 14% in BC, which correlates with the produce-access gap seen earlier: 13.4% of NL adults ate 5+ servings of fruit and vegetables daily versus a national average of 21.6%.

Other, unexplained

46%

The largest single slice, and the honest part of the model. It likely includes genetics and biology, which this model doesn’t isolate as its own category, plus measurement error, chance, and interactions between factors a simple additive model can’t capture.

A separate research model (McGinnis et al., 2002) estimated genetic predisposition alone at around 30% of premature death risk, suggesting genetics makes up a real part of this slice.

Summary

Newfoundland and Labrador and British Columbia sit at opposite ends of Canada’s health spectrum, with NL scoring worse on every risk factor and outcome measured here, gaps that trace back to lower income and higher rurality rather than healthcare access, which is roughly equally strained in both provinces; and published research on what drives variation in health outcomes generally (Bauer et al., 2015) backs up that pattern, finding that social and economic conditions explain more of the variance than health behaviors, which explain more than clinical care or the physical environment, with genetics and other unmeasured factors making up the largest remaining share — a reminder that no single-factor story, including this one, fully explains why two provinces end up so far apart.

(2015, County Health Rankings model validation), McGinnis, Williams-Russo & Knickman (Health Affairs, 2002).
